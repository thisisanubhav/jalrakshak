# Jharkhand drinking-water problem: evidence review

## Evidence-weighted finding

Jharkhand has a real drinking-water monitoring problem, but it is not accurately described as “villages lack any testing system” or “TDS reveals contamination.” The strongest current evidence is a combination of:

1. **heterogeneous source risks**—microbial, geogenic and mining/industrial concerns vary by source and district;
2. **uneven surveillance capacity**—the official 2024 functionality assessment found large district differences in Field Test Kit (FTK) availability and village skilled manpower;
3. **service-quality variability**—working, adequate, regular and assessed-quality tap service differed substantially by district;
4. **a need to connect repeatable field observations to existing confirmation and remedial-action systems**, not replace those systems.

## Rural source and service context

The 2024 JJM Jharkhand assessment reports a state snapshot of 15,487 groundwater sources, 6,872 surface-water sources and 212 springs, alongside a rapidly expanding piped-water programme. In its sample of 555 Har Ghar Jal villages and 6,660 households, 97.7% of sampled households had a tap connection but 88.9% had a working connection; only 72.0% received at least 55 litres per capita per day. These estimates apply to the assessment’s sampled Har Ghar Jal villages, not all rural households or standalone handpumps ([JJM Jharkhand report, pp. 19–27](https://jaljeevanmission.gov.in/sites/default/files/2025-12/FHTC_Jharkhand_State%20Report.pdf)).

District variation was material. For example, the same assessment reported working-connection proportions of 48.2% in Pakur, 68.4% in Giridih and 69.7% in Sahibganj, compared with 100% in several sampled districts. This demonstrates operational heterogeneity, not a water-contaminant prevalence estimate ([JJM Jharkhand report, pp. 23–24](https://jaljeevanmission.gov.in/sites/default/files/2025-12/FHTC_Jharkhand_State%20Report.pdf)).

## Water quality evidence

### Official programme evidence

For assessed household tap water, the 2024 functionality study tested on-site pH and sent E. coli and total-coliform parameters to NABL-accredited laboratories. Reported district results ranged from 45.4% meeting that assessment’s “potable” definition in Simdega and 49.4% in Dumka to 100% in Sahibganj. Because only the stated parameters were tested in this assessment, these figures must not be read as full IS 10500 compliance or as a map of all sources ([JJM Jharkhand report, pp. 25–26](https://jaljeevanmission.gov.in/sites/default/files/2025-12/FHTC_Jharkhand_State%20Report.pdf)).

National programme inventories tell a different but compatible story: as of 7 December 2022, Jharkhand reported 57 iron-affected and 2 fluoride-affected rural habitations, with both fluoride habitations covered by interim community purification provision ([Ministry of Jal Shakti parliamentary answer, 12 December 2022](https://www.pib.gov.in/Pressreleaseshare.aspx?PRID=1882782)). As of 13 December 2023 the state again reported two fluoride-affected habitations and none listed for arsenic in that table ([Ministry of Jal Shakti parliamentary answer, 14 December 2023](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1986268)). These are administrative inventories of habitations classified under the programme; they do not establish that only those locations ever show localized exceedances.

### Local and peer-reviewed evidence

- **Koderma/Chandwara:** A 2022 study across 39 villages reported TDS of 75.3–1,272 mg/L, with 36% of samples above 500 mg/L; iron of 0.02–6.64 mg/L, with 28.7% above the cited acceptable limit; and high turbidity in 15.4% of samples, often associated with iron. This establishes a local problem, not statewide prevalence ([Singh et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13201-022-01587-6)).
- **East Singhbhum mining region:** A 2018 groundwater study attributed observed Cd, Pb and Cr burdens to a combination of lithology and anthropogenic mining/mineral-processing inputs. This supports targeted investigation in mining-affected areas, not a claim that mining contaminates every source ([Singh, Ramanathan & Subramanian, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemosphere.2018.04.060)).
- **Ranchi area microbial evidence:** A 2015 study of mixed sources reported coliform in 53% and faecal streptococci in 20% of sampled waters. It is old and local, but it demonstrates why an ionic sensor cannot stand in for microbial surveillance ([Kerketta et al., 2015](https://microbiologyjournal.org/bacteriological-analysis-of-drinking-water-from-different-sources-in-and-around-ranchi-jharkhand/)).

### Parameters that the evidence does and does not support

| Issue | Jharkhand-specific evidence | Interpretation for JalRakshak |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | Official 2022 inventory and local Koderma evidence | Real in some places; requires iron-specific FTK/lab analysis. EC/turbidity cannot identify iron. |
| Fluoride | Two affected habitations in 2022–23 administrative inventory; localized literature reports elsewhere/earlier | Target by current official risk mapping and local validation; EC cannot detect fluoride. |
| Arsenic | No affected habitation in cited 2023 programme table, though older/local studies report occurrences | Do not market arsenic detection; investigate only through validated arsenic methods and current local evidence. |
| Nitrate | Local/hydrochemical literature indicates possible occurrences | Not quantified statewide here; requires parameter-specific testing. |
| Salinity/TDS | Local Koderma exceedances; EC is relevant to mineralization | Suitable for pilot measurement/anomaly research, not a health diagnosis. |
| Turbidity | Local Koderma results and operational relevance | Potential future parameter with validated instrument; not proof of microbial contamination. |
| Microbial contamination | Official 2024 assessment includes E. coli/coliform; older Ranchi study found contamination | High-priority blind spot for V1 EC hardware; retain H2S/approved bacteriological/lab route. |
| Mining-related metals | Peer-reviewed East Singhbhum evidence | Targeted lab programme is appropriate; generic sensor is not. |

## Seasonal change

WHO advises timing/frequency to account for seasonal variation; microbial loading and turbidity may rise in wetter periods, while some groundwater chemicals are more stable ([WHO small-supply guidelines, 2024](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240088740)). The CGWB monitors shallow groundwater in pre- and post-monsoon cycles, reinforcing that a one-off reading cannot define a source baseline ([CGWB Jharkhand pre-monsoon bulletin, 2024](https://gwdata.cgwb.gov.in/download/Bulletin/Jharkhand%20GWQ%20Bulletin%20Pre-monsoon%202024.pdf)). JalRakshak should therefore compare like seasons and record rainfall/season context.

## Surveillance-capacity evidence

The most direct evidence for a workflow gap is not absence of government infrastructure; it is uneven availability at village level. In the 2024 JJM assessment sample:

- 89.6% of villages reported a VWSC/Paani Samiti;
- only 43.4% reported skilled O&M manpower;
- FTK availability ranged from 0% in sampled Sahibganj villages and 8.6% in Hazaribag to 100% in Khunti, with many districts between those extremes.

([JJM Jharkhand report, pp. 30–33](https://jaljeevanmission.gov.in/sites/default/files/2025-12/FHTC_Jharkhand_State%20Report.pdf)).

These findings justify a field pilot about workflow reliability and measurement quality. They do not prove that an additional app is the solution.

## Conflicting evidence and limits

1. **Administrative inventories versus research samples:** state-reported “affected habitation” counts are much smaller than the footprint implied by some local studies. They use different sampling frames, dates, classifications and purposes; neither should be used to invalidate the other.
2. **Tap assessment versus source water:** the 2024 JJM assessment sampled household tap water in Har Ghar Jal villages, while JalRakshak’s MVP emphasizes handpumps/wells/borewells. Results are not interchangeable.
3. **Age:** several contaminant studies are 8–11 years old. They establish plausibility and localized history, not current operational status.
4. **Selection:** mining-region and known-problem studies intentionally sample higher-risk contexts and cannot estimate state prevalence.
5. **Incomplete public performance evidence:** WQMIS exposes reports and workflows, but this review found no independent Jharkhand evaluation of referral turnaround, data completeness, offline failure rates or user burden.
6. **No causal evidence for JalRakshak:** no reviewed source shows that offline EC logging improves lab confirmation or corrective action in Jharkhand. That is the pilot hypothesis.

## What remains unknown

- What percentage of intended Jharkhand FTK users test at the required frequency and successfully upload results?
- What are median times from suspicious FTK result to sample collection, laboratory receipt, result and remedial action?
- Is connectivity, reagent availability, training, transport, calibration, incentives or accountability the dominant bottleneck?
- Does Jhar-Jal already work offline and retain longitudinal source history adequately?
- Can low-cost EC hardware achieve useful repeatability under Jal Sahiya/community-worker conditions?
- Are EC anomalies correlated with actionable laboratory findings strongly enough to justify device and maintenance cost?

These questions require interviews and a paired-measurement pilot, not more desk research.
